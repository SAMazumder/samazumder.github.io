I had some time over my vacation to read a treatise by [Aisha Ahmad](https://www.utsc.utoronto.ca/polisci/aisha-ahmad) (incidently, my former TA at U of T) titled: ["Jihad & Co.: Black Markets and Islamist Power"](https://www.politics.utoronto.ca/research-publications/faculty-publications/jihad-co-%E2%80%93-black-markets-and-islamist-power).

To my understanding, the central argument of her work can be summarized by the following passage:

> [...] [T]wo specific economic logics that explain [...] business Islamist cooperation: the need for higher trust and lower costs. First, in the long term, the business community adopts an Islamist identity to increase social trust and access to markets, which sets the stage for an Islamist takeover; second, in the short term, business elites make the strategic calculation to shift support to Islamist groups that provide them with better protection at a lower price, thus funding the Islamists to seize control of the state. These two rational, economic motivations lay the foundation of the business-Islamist alliance on which the Islamist proto-state can be built.

Reading through her work, my mind started reverting back to a game theory class I took during my undergrad at TMU. As such, I am endeavouring to put her work and my thoughts about it under a game-theoretical foundation. It has of course been quite some time since I've done any game theory work and everything I'm writing here consists of unordered thoughts put into a (vaguely) comprehensible order. As such, I make no claims to have produced a complete formal model of Ahmad's argument. Rather, this is my attempt to translate some of the mechanisms in her work into the language of game theory.

# A Game-Theoretic Analysis of Islamist Proto-State Formation

## 1. The Basic Idea

Like many games, we can express the above argument as a relatively simple game of **security provision and coordination**. In a weak or fragmented state, businesses cannot rely fully on a central government to protect property, enforce contracts, or guarantee the movement of goods. Instead, they may have to interact with competing armed groups that provide varying levels of security at varying costs.

The game-theoretic question is therefore perhaps: **Under what conditions will economic players collectively shift their support from fragmented security providers to a single organization capable of offering broader and cheaper security?**

The important word here is *collectively*. A business may prefer a particular security provider in isolation, but the value of that provider can depend on whether other businesses also support it. A security provider that can credibly operate across several territories, for example, becomes more valuable as more businesses use it.

Indeed, we assume that both businesses and armed groups are rational. In particular, we assume that rationality can emerge, at least on average, from ordinary strategic incentives. This does not require that every individual actor consciously calculate a utility function; rather, the model assumes that behavior is sufficiently responsive to incentives that these strategic relationships can emerge at the aggregate level.

---

## 2. Players

Consider a simplified environment with two types of players:

- A set of **business actors**, indexed by $i=1,\ldots,N$.
- Two competing **security providers**, $A$ and $B$.

Without loss of generality, suppose that provider $A$ represents an ethnic or tribal armed faction whose support is concentrated within a particular community, and that provider $B$ represents a faction capable of offering security across several communities.

Each business therefore has a choice over which organization to support, contract with, or otherwise accommodate:

$$
s_i \in \{A,B\}.
$$

This is a simplification. In an actual civil war, businesses may simultaneously maintain relationships with several armed groups, attempt to remain neutral, or have little meaningful choice over who controls their territory. The model abstracts from these complications in order to isolate the strategic mechanism.

---

## 3. The Business's Payoff

Let the payoff to business $i$ be

$$
U_i = \pi_i - c_i - t_{s_i} - r_{s_i},
$$

where:

- $\pi_i$ = gross profit from trade,
- $c_i$ = ordinary operating costs,
- $t_{s_i}$ = payment or tax demanded by the security provider,
- $r_{s_i}$ = expected cost of insecurity, disruption, or expropriation.

Thus, a rational business player chooses the arrangement that gives it the greatest expected economic return. That is, a security provider becomes attractive to the business when

$$
t_{s_i}+r_{s_i}
$$

is sufficiently low relative to its competitors. This captures the central economic intuition: **security has a price, and that price affects political competition.**

However, this is only the starting point. If the costs of security provision were entirely independent of what other businesses did, this would be a simple choice problem rather than a particularly interesting game. The game-theoretic element enters when the cost and quality of a security provider depend partly on the number of businesses supporting it.

We can therefore write the payoff more generally as

$$
U_i(s_i,q)=\pi_i-c_i-t_{s_i}(q)-r_{s_i}(q),
$$

where $q$ represents the proportion of businesses supporting provider $B$.

---

## 4. Why Fragmentation Matters

Now, suppose that security provider $A$ is associated primarily with one ethnic or tribal constituency. A business that operates across several territories may therefore face several different versions of this security provider across those territories:

$$
A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_k.
$$

Its effective security cost can be represented as

$$
C_F=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(t_j+r_j)+T(k),
$$

where $T(k)$ represents the additional transaction costs generated by fragmentation.

The subscript $F$ denotes a **fragmented security market**. Fragmentation creates costs beyond the direct price of protection. A business player may have to negotiate separately with different factions, maintain multiple relationships, anticipate checkpoints, learn different rules, and account for the possibility that an agreement with one faction is not recognized by another. These are transaction costs.

We assume:

$$
T'(k)>0.
$$

That is, all else equal, dealing with a larger number of independent security providers increases the costs of doing business.

Now suppose an alternative provider can operate across the same territories and charge a single effective cost $C_B$:

$$
C_B=t_B+r_B+T_B.
$$

The alternative becomes economically attractive when

$$
C_B<C_F.
$$

To the business player, the political significance of this inequality is:

> **An organization that can provide security across existing social boundaries can potentially outcompete organizations whose support is restricted by those boundaries.**

This is one of the places where Ahmad's argument about Islamist organizations becomes particularly interesting. A cross-cutting organization may be able to sell security to a much larger pool of businesses than an organization whose protection network is restricted by clan, tribal, or ethnic boundaries.

---

## 5. Where Religious Identity Enters the Model

Religious identity enters the model by affecting the **cost of cooperation**.

Let

$$
\tau_{ij}
$$

represent the level of trust between business actors $i$ and $j$. Here, a shared identity may increase trust:

$$
\frac{\partial \tau_{ij}}{\partial I}>0,
$$

where $I$ represents a shared identity or institutional affiliation.

Higher trust can then reduce transaction costs:

$$
\frac{\partial C}{\partial \tau}<0.
$$

The resulting mechanism is:

$$
\text{shared identity}
\rightarrow
\text{greater trust}
\rightarrow
\text{lower transaction costs}
\rightarrow
\text{greater economic cooperation}.
$$

As identified in Ahmad's work, this is a conditional claim. Shared identity does not automatically produce trust, and religious identity is not inherently more effective than every other form of identity. Its relevance depends on the particular social and institutional environment.

In the cases Ahmad examines, Islamic identity can function as a cross-cutting form of social capital, allowing business relationships to extend across clan, ethnic, or tribal boundaries. The important point for the model is therefore not that Islamism mechanically produces trust, but that a shared identity can alter the structure of the network through which economic cooperation takes place.

---

## 6. The Security Provider's Problem

Now, the security provider also faces a strategic choice.

Let its revenue be

$$
R=tN,
$$

where:

- $t$ = extraction per business,
- $N$ = number of businesses supporting the organization.

Increasing $t$ raises revenue per business but may reduce $N$.

Thus,

$$
R(t)=tN(t),
$$

with

$$
\frac{dN}{dt}<0.
$$

The security provider therefore faces a familiar revenue-maximization problem.

If extraction becomes excessive, businesses may:

- reduce economic activity,
- evade payments,
- move to another provider,
- finance a rival,
- or collectively resist.

The provider's optimal extraction rate is therefore

$$
t^*=\arg\max_t \; tN(t).
$$

This gives the armed group an incentive to maintain enough security and economic activity to preserve its revenue base.

More realistically, however, the provider also has to bear the cost of maintaining the capacity necessary to provide security. We can therefore write its payoff as

$$
\Pi=tN(t,K)-C(K),
$$

where $K$ represents organizational capacity and $C(K)$ is the cost of maintaining that capacity.

This introduces a second strategic tradeoff. The organization must balance extraction against the capacity required to make its security provision credible. A provider that extracts too much may lose its economic base; a provider that invests too little in security may fail to attract businesses in the first place.

---

## 7. The Coordination Problem

The most interesting part of the model is that individual businesses may prefer a change in the security system while still being reluctant to make the change individually.

Consider two businesses, $1$ and $2$.

Each can either:

- remain with the incumbent provider $A$, or
- support alternative provider $B$.

A stylized payoff matrix might look like this:

| | Business 2: $A$ | Business 2: $B$ |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Business 1: $A$** | $(3,3)$ | $(1,2)$ |
| **Business 1: $B$** | $(2,1)$ | $(4,4)$ |

The interpretation is:

- If both remain with $A$, they receive a tolerable but inefficient equilibrium: $(3,3)$.
- If one switches alone, that business bears some switching costs and receives a relatively poor payoff.
- If both switch, both receive the highest payoff: $(4,4)$.

This is a simple **coordination game**.

There are two Nash equilibria: $(A,A)$ and $(B,B)$. The important point is that $(B,B)$ is collectively preferable to $(A,A)$, but a business that expects the other business to remain with $A$ has an incentive to remain with $A$ as well.

For example,

$$
U_1(B,A)=2<3=U_1(A,A),
$$

but

$$
U_1(B,B)=4>1=U_1(A,B).
$$

Thus, the problem is not exactly a Prisoner's Dilemma. In a Prisoner's Dilemma, individually rational behavior produces a collectively inefficient equilibrium. Here, instead, the problem is that there are **multiple equilibria**, and actors may be reluctant to move from an established equilibrium to a potentially superior one.

This distinction is important because it gives us a way to understand why an apparently superior security provider might nevertheless struggle to attract support. The problem is not necessarily that businesses do not want the alternative. It may be that no individual business wants to be the first to make the switch.

---

## 8. The Role of a Credible Alternative

An organization becomes politically important when it can transform this coordination problem.

Suppose the organization can credibly demonstrate that it can:

1. provide security,
2. enforce agreements,
3. operate across existing factional boundaries,
4. maintain access to commercial networks, and
5. impose a sufficiently predictable level of taxation or extraction.

Then the expected payoff from switching increases.

Let

$$
\Delta U_i=U_i(B)-U_i(A).
$$

A business switches when

$$
\Delta U_i>0.
$$

But expectations about what other businesses will do matter as well.

If business $i$ expects many other businesses to switch, the cost of switching falls because the new organization becomes more viable. We can represent this as

$$
U_i(B)=u_B+\alpha q,
$$

where $q$ is the expected proportion of businesses supporting $B$ and $\alpha>0$ captures the network effect.

Then:

$$
\frac{\partial U_i(B)}{\partial q}>0.
$$

This produces a **positive feedback mechanism**.

Suppose, for simplicity, that

$$
U_i(A)=u_A.
$$

Business $i$ therefore chooses $B$ when

$$
u_B+\alpha q>u_A.
$$

The critical threshold is consequently

$$
q^*=\frac{u_A-u_B}{\alpha}.
$$

Thus, when

$$
q>q^*,
$$

support for $B$ becomes individually rational.

This is the mechanism through which a relatively small initial advantage can potentially become self-reinforcing.

---

## 9. The Tipping-Point Dynamic

Suppose there is a critical threshold $q^*$.

If expected support for $B$ is below $q^*$, businesses remain cautious:

$$
q<q^*
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\text{continued support for }A.
$$

But if expectations cross the threshold,

$$
q>q^*
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\text{increasing support for }B.
$$

This produces a tipping-point dynamic:

$$
\boxed{
\text{lower expected cost}
\rightarrow
\text{more support}
\rightarrow
\text{greater capacity}
\rightarrow
\text{better security}
\rightarrow
\text{lower expected cost}
}
$$

The process can therefore be self-reinforcing.

This helps explain why political change can appear sudden even when the underlying economic and social relationships have developed gradually. The static payoff matrix is only a simplified representation; the actual process is dynamic, with businesses, armed groups, and other political actors repeatedly updating their expectations.

---

## 10. From Security Provider to Proto-State

The final step is a transition from a competitive security organization to a proto-state.

Suppose an organization gains enough revenue to increase its territorial and administrative capacity:

$$
K=f(R),
\qquad f'(R)>0.
$$

Here $K$ represents organizational capacity.

Higher capacity can allow the organization to provide more reliable security and administration, which increases the number of businesses willing to support it:

$$
K\uparrow
\Rightarrow
U_i(B)\uparrow
\Rightarrow
N_B\uparrow
\Rightarrow
R_B\uparrow.
$$

The resulting feedback loop is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{economic support}
\rightarrow
\text{organizational capacity}
\rightarrow
\text{security}
\rightarrow
\text{greater economic support}
}
$$

But organizational capacity alone does not make an organization a proto-state. There must also be some degree of territorial consolidation and political authority.

We can therefore think of the transition as:

$$
\text{revenue}
\rightarrow
\text{organizational capacity}
\rightarrow
\text{territorial control}
\rightarrow
\text{dominant security provision}
\rightarrow
\text{governance functions}.
$$

At sufficiently high levels of territorial and organizational consolidation, the organization may begin performing functions normally associated with a state:

- taxation,
- dispute resolution,
- policing,
- regulation,
- provision of public order,
- territorial administration.

At that point, the organization can reasonably be described analytically as a **proto-state**, even though it may lack international recognition or the institutional characteristics of a conventional state.

The important distinction is therefore between an organization that merely provides protection and one that has begun to consolidate political authority over a territory.

---

## 11. The Equilibrium

The model suggests that the emergence of a proto-state does not necessarily require businesses and armed groups to share the same ideology.

They can have different preferences.

The business actor may primarily want:

$$
\max \; \text{profit} - \text{security costs},
$$

while the armed organization may want:

$$
\max \; \text{political power} + \text{revenue} - \text{organizational costs}.
$$

Their interests overlap when the organization can provide security at a sufficiently attractive cost.

Thus, an alliance can emerge even when the participants have very different ultimate objectives.

In game-theoretic terms, this is a **mutually beneficial strategic relationship**, rather than necessarily an ideological convergence.

The important qualification is that the economic relationship can itself alter the political environment. As businesses coordinate around a particular security provider, that provider's organizational capacity and political leverage increase. Economic choices can therefore become politically consequential even when the original motivation behind those choices is primarily economic.

---

## 12. An Interpretation of the "Islamist Discount"

The concept of an "Islamist discount" can be restated as a **cross-factional security advantage**.

Suppose a tribal faction can effectively sell security to only $N_T$ businesses, while a cross-cutting organization can sell security to $N_I>N_T$.

The broader organization can potentially spread its fixed organizational costs across a larger revenue base:

$$
\text{average cost}
=
\frac{F}{N},
$$

where $F$ represents fixed costs of maintaining the organization.

If

$$
N_I>N_T,
$$

then, other things equal,

$$
\frac{F}{N_I}<\frac{F}{N_T}.
$$

This creates a potential cost advantage and is dependent on the ability of an organization to **expand the market over which it can credibly provide security and collect revenue**.

Religious identity may be one mechanism through which that expansion occurs. In Ahmad's account, Islamic identity can provide a cross-cutting social identity that allows relationships to extend beyond narrower clan or ethnic networks. It can therefore affect the size of the market available to the security provider.

I note that the model does not imply that Islamist identity is economically irrelevant. Rather, it treats the economic effect of that identity as operating through mechanisms such as trust, network breadth, legitimacy, and expectations.

---

## Some Thoughts

The above model provides a possible explanation for **variation in organizational success**. I have applied this model based on the information gleamed and interpreted from Ahmad's research. However, it is not immediately clear to be if an Islamist identity by itself guarantees a (1) broad security market, (2) credible cross-factional networks, or (3) sufficient organizational capacity. 

Perhaps the goal of this analysis is to determine how rational actors can create political order can emerge from anarchy. That is, when formal institutions collapse, individuals still have to solve repeated problems of:

$$
\text{trust},\quad
\text{security},\quad
\text{contract enforcement},\quad
\text{coordination}.
$$

Actors who can solve these problems more effectively can accumulate economic and political support as noted by Ahmad. As such, perhaps we can extend Ahmad's central question
> *Why do people support an Islamist organization?*

to the game-theoretic problem of **coordination, collective action, and endogenous political order** as follows:

> **When does an alternative security provider become sufficiently credible and economically attractive that individual decisions to support it become mutually reinforcing?**
